# Suspicious Activity Reporting (SAR)

## Purpose
When the Bank detects known or suspected criminal activity involving funds of $5,000 or more (or lower thresholds in certain cases), or a transaction that has no apparent lawful purpose, it must file a Suspicious Activity Report with the relevant financial intelligence unit (e.g. FinCEN in the U.S.), generally within 30 calendar days of detection.

## Suspicious Activity Indicators
Common red flags that feed the SAR decision process include:
- Structuring — breaking a large transaction into smaller ones to avoid reporting thresholds.
- Rapid movement of funds through an account with no clear business purpose ("funnel account" behavior).
- Transactions inconsistent with the customer's stated business or expected activity profile.
- Use of multiple accounts or identities to obscure the true party to a transaction.
- Activity matching known typologies for the customer's product or geography (e.g. trade-based laundering indicators for cross-border trade finance customers).

## SAR Process Requirements
1. **Detection** — via transaction monitoring alerts, employee referral, law enforcement inquiry, or other means.
2. **Investigation** — an analyst reviews the alert, gathers supporting documentation, and determines whether the activity is suspicious.
3. **Decision and filing** — a decision to file or not file is documented; if filing, the SAR is submitted within the regulatory deadline, and underlying records are retained per policy (typically five years).
4. **Confidentiality** — SAR filings, and the fact that one was filed, are strictly confidential and must not be disclosed to the subject of the report.

## Relevance to New Product / Change Assessments
Any change should be evaluated for whether it creates new patterns of activity that existing SAR-relevant monitoring scenarios would fail to catch, and whether investigators have the tools and data access needed to investigate alerts arising from the new product or process.
